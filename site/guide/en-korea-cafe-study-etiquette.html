Studying in cafés in Korea: what is normal and what is not

Yes, you can study in cafés in Korea, and plenty of people do. There is even a word for it: 카공 (kagong), short for 카페 공부, "café study". What changes from place to place is how long you are welcome and how much of the table you can take up. This guide explains what is ordinary, what tends to annoy people, and what is different in an unmanned café such as Mingle, near Konkuk University in Seoul.

Is it okay to study in a café in Korea?

Yes. In most Korean cafés, a laptop and a textbook on the table will not surprise anyone. Students revise there, freelancers answer email there, and people preparing for big exams treat a favourite café as a second desk.

It is also something people argue about. A café owner pays rent on every seat, and a customer who keeps a four-person table all afternoon on one small coffee is a real cost to a small shop. So you will hear the word 카공족 (kagong-jok, roughly "the café-study crowd") said with irritation as often as with affection. Both sides have a point, and remembering that is most of the etiquette.

Because of this, the rules differ by café. Some are plainly set up for it, with long shared tables and outlets along the wall. Others put up signs: one order per person, a time limit when it is busy, no laptops at certain tables, or no studying at all. Look around the counter and on the tables before you unpack.

The basics nearly everyone agrees on

None of this is written into law, but you will rarely go wrong if you follow it.

  • Buy something, and buy again if you stay long. Your order is what pays for the seat. Nobody will tell you when a drink has run out of time, but ordering a second one after a few hours is the usual courtesy.
  • Take only the space you need. One person spread across a large table during the lunch rush is exactly the picture people complain about.
  • Do not hold a seat while you are away for hours. A bag on a chair while you step away for a few minutes is normal. A laptop left to guard a table while you go out for dinner is not.
  • Keep calls outside. Typing and quiet conversation are fine. Phone calls, video meetings and anything played through a speaker are not.
  • Be modest with power. Use the outlet by your seat, leave the café's own plugs alone, and do not run a cable across a walkway.
  • Clear your table. In most Korean cafés you carry your tray and cups to a return counter yourself.

What surprises newcomers most?

Usually it is how much people trust the room. You will see someone leave a phone or a laptop on the table and walk off for several minutes. It is common, but it is a habit and not a guarantee, and it makes less sense in a place with no staff at all.

A few words that turn up on café signs are worth recognising:

  • 1인 1음료 (il-in il-eumnyo): one drink per person
  • 콘센트 (konsenteu): power outlet
  • 반납 (bannap): return, meaning where trays and cups go
  • 노스터디존 (no-seuteodi-jon): no-study zone
  • 무인 (mu-in): unmanned, self-service

If there is no sign and you are unsure, ask. "여기서 공부해도 돼요?" (yeogiseo gongbuhaedo dwaeyo?) means "Is it okay to study here?"

One more thing: a 스터디카페 (study café) is a different business. It is a quiet room where you pay for desk time, not a coffee shop. When a classmate suggests one, expect silence and an hourly fee.

What changes in an unmanned café?

In an unmanned café there is nobody to ask and nobody to clear up after you, so the same manners carry more weight.

Take Mingle as an example. It is open 24 hours a day, every day, and its seats have no time limit. You order at a kiosk, which has an English option and takes cards and mobile payment only. Window seats have power outlets, and there are tables and sofa seats too. The visit page lists the address and the steps.

No time limit does not mean no etiquette. With no one on duty, tidying up is entirely yours: sort cups and packaging into the bins and push your chair in, because the next person sees exactly what you left. Carry your valuables when you step out, since nobody is keeping an eye on your seat. Do not leave things on a chair to hold it for long; other customers have no one to ask whether it is free. And if something is broken or messy, send a photo through the KakaoTalk channel, because an unmanned store cannot take phone calls.

A simple way to read any café

When you walk into a café you do not know, three looks tell you most of what you need. Look at the tables: are other people working, or is everyone talking over cake? Look at the walls and the counter for signs. Then look at how full it is. A half-empty café on a weekday afternoon and a packed one on a Saturday are different places, even at the same address.

Then fit your plan to what you see. A short session with a laptop is welcome almost anywhere that has outlets. A whole day is something to save for cafés built for it, or for places that say clearly that there is no time limit.

FAQ

Is it rude to study in a café in Korea?

No. Studying in cafés, known as kagong (카공), is common in Korea. It becomes a problem only when someone ignores a café's posted rules or holds a table for hours on a single order.

What does kagong mean?

Kagong (카공) is short for 카페 공부, which means studying in a café. People who do it regularly are sometimes called kagong-jok (카공족).

How long can I stay in a Korean café on one drink?

There is no single rule in Korea. Each café decides for itself, and some post a time limit when they are busy. A common courtesy is to order again if you stay for several hours.

Can I leave my laptop on the table when I step away?

Many people in Korea do so for a short break, but it is a habit, not a guarantee. In an unmanned café such as Mingle, where no staff are present, take your valuables with you.

Does Mingle limit how long I can study there?

No. Mingle, a 24-hour unmanned dessert café near Konkuk University, has no time limit on seats. When you leave, sort your cups and packaging into the bins and tidy your seat.
